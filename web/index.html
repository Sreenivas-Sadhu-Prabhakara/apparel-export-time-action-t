How the dates are worked out

Three passes over the milestone chain.

Backwards from the ship date. The last milestone finishes on the ship date. Everything before it finishes the last day it can and still leave its successors their full lead time. Those are the planned dates, and they are the sheet you send out.

Forwards from the purchase-order date. The same chain counted the other way gives the earliest each milestone could run. The gap between the two is that milestone's slack, and if the forward pass lands past the ship date the order cannot be made in the time it has been given — the tool says so, and by how many days.

Forwards again from what actually happened. Where a completion date has been recorded it is used as it stands; everything after it is pushed to the first day it can now run, and nothing unfinished is allowed to sit in the past. That is what moves the inspection and the vessel booking when finishing runs late.

Critical path and slack

The critical path is traced, not guessed: from the milestone that sets the earliest possible ship date, back through whichever predecessor is the reason each one cannot start sooner. It always comes out as one connected chain from a milestone that waits on nothing to the one the ship date hangs on. Lose a day anywhere on it and you lose a day off the ship date.

Milestones off that chain have slack. Slack is shown in the milestone's own unit: working days for a factory stage, calendar days for transit, because that is the unit in which the days can actually be spent.

A backward plan has no spare days in it. Every date is a last possible date, so a milestone that finishes after its own date moves the ship date — unless the days the factory is shut happen to cover it. The slack figure tells you how much cushion the order was booked with, not how much you can afford to lose at a milestone.

The lead times this build starts with are trade practice for a mid-volume programme, not rules, not anybody's published standard, and not advice. They move with the buyer, the fabric, the season, the mill's order book and the shipping line. Replace every one of them with figures from your own last few orders before you plan against them.

Assumptions, stated plainly
  • A milestone finishes before the next one starts. There is no overlap: a chain where sewing starts while cutting is still running should carry a shorter sewing lead time, or be split into two milestones.
  • A lead time counts the first day. A one-day milestone finishes the day it starts.
  • A milestone counted in working days never starts or finishes on a day the factory is shut. One counted in calendar days can.
  • A holiday falling on the weekly off costs one day, not two.
  • A recorded completion date is taken as it stands, even on a closed day: a shift may well have run, and the tool records what happened rather than correcting it.
  • A milestone due today is not yet overdue. It becomes overdue the morning after.
  • Without a purchase-order date the plan is still worked out, from the last day the order could have been placed, and no slack or feasibility figure is given.
